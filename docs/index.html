Technical memorandum

A base-out simulation model for baseball outcomes, and its out-of-sample validation

Eli Witz May–August 2026 Source code and full write-up

Summary

This memorandum describes a Monte Carlo model that estimates the joint distribution of runs scored in a baseball game from individual player rates, and reports the out-of-sample testing used to assess it. The model was built to price sixteen families of contracts on a regulated binary options exchange, six of which it quoted continuously for two months.

The exchange price provides an independent benchmark forecast for every contract, and every contract settles against an observed outcome, so the model can be scored against a competing forecast rather than against its own residuals. On 163,777 scored contracts covering 786 games, the model did not outperform that benchmark: its Brier score on game-level markets was 0.1891 against the market's 0.1883, a difference whose game-clustered confidence interval contains zero. Performance varies by segment, and section 3 reports the breakdown.

1.Model structure

Prices depend on tail probabilities rather than expectations. A contract on total runs requires P(total > 8.5); a contract on margin of victory requires the distribution of a difference between two correlated quantities. A regression fitted to expected runs supplies neither, because the mapping from a conditional mean to a tail probability depends on distributional shape the regression does not estimate.

The model therefore simulates. A half-inning is treated as a Markov chain on 24 base-out states, being the eight configurations of occupied bases crossed with three out counts. Each plate appearance draws one of nine outcomes from a distribution specific to the batter and the opposing pitcher, and the pair (state, outcome) determines a distribution over the resulting state and any runs scored. Repeated simulation yields the joint distribution of home and away runs, from which all prices are computed as functionals, which keeps them mutually consistent.

What is being built is a compound distribution: a random number of events, each of random size, aggregated into a total whose tail governs the quantity of interest. Simulation is used rather than analytic convolution because the summands are neither independent nor identically distributed — the base-out state couples successive plate appearances, and the batter changes at each one.

Eleven further effects are layered over this core, each estimated separately: a gradient-boosted discrete-time hazard for starting-pitcher removal, a McFadden conditional logit for relief-pitcher selection, times-through-the-order penalties, park and umpire effects, platoon splits, and others.

2.Parameter estimation

Each batter requires nine outcome probabilities against each pitcher. Observed rates are unusable directly: a hitter with 150 plate appearances has a strikeout rate whose standard error exceeds most of the differences the model must resolve.

Rates are estimated hierarchically. Each player's rate is shrunk toward a prior mean that is itself a regression on observable covariates, in a Fay–Herriot small-area specification fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Posterior draws can be propagated through the simulation rather than collapsed to posterior means, so that parameter uncertainty is represented alongside process uncertainty.

The weight on a player's own record is his playing time over playing time plus a variance ratio, so a hitter with a handful of games is pulled most of the way toward what the covariate model predicts for him, while one with a full season barely moves. Two points are worth noting. The variance components are estimated jointly with the rates rather than plugged in, so the uncertainty in that weight is itself carried. And because the model's output is a tail probability, propagating the posterior matters: a tail probability evaluated at the posterior mean is not the posterior mean of the tail probability, and treating them as equal produces forecasts that are systematically overconfident.

3.Out-of-sample results

Forecasts are scored with the Brier score, a proper scoring rule, so that a forecaster minimizes expected loss only by reporting its true belief. Log loss is computed alongside as a second proper rule with different tail sensitivity. The Brier score decomposes into reliability and resolution — calibration and discrimination — which is what makes it diagnostic rather than merely comparative, since a model that always forecasts the base rate is perfectly calibrated and useless.

Aggregate parity conceals substantial variation across contract families. Figure 1 gives the difference in Brier score, model minus market, by family.

Eleven of the sixteen priced families carried enough scored contracts in this window to support a clustered interval; the remainder are omitted rather than reported at a precision the sample does not support. Of the eleven, four are significantly worse and one significantly better. The exception, home runs recorded, is also the largest by volume, and three further tests agree: area under the ROC curve of 0.762 against the market's 0.756, lower calibration error, and a direct comparison in which the model's side wins 55.1% of the 2,962 contracts where model and market take opposite positions. That last test applied to game-level markets returns 49.2%, so on those markets disagreement with the price was marginally worse than a coin flip.

The weakness in game totals concentrates at high lines rather than spreading evenly across the family, which locates it as a specific modeling defect rather than as evidence that the family is efficiently priced.

4.Sensitivity to the base-running assumption

Base-running advancement is the largest single lever on simulated scoring. The published code constructs the transition tables three ways and measures each against observed league scoring, rather than assuming the simplification is immaterial.

The deterministic treatment understates scoring by 21%, being unable to represent a double play, a sacrifice fly, or a runner scoring from second on a single. Eight sourced constants recover 68% of the shortfall; the residual 6% consists of rare events that no compact parameterization represents.

Most of the recovered difference comes from conditioning these rates on the out count. A runner scores from second on a single 39% of the time with none out and 80% with two out, since with two out he advances on contact. The commonly cited single figure of 63% is an average over out states, and applying it uniformly — as an earlier version of this code did — biases the run distribution in opposite directions depending on the state.

5.Rejected model changes

Accepted changes are selected on having worked and so carry little information about process. The following were each implemented, measured against a criterion fixed before the test, and abandoned.

Position players pitching
The maximum attainable effect was computed before implementation, as the affected share of plate appearances multiplied by the largest plausible rate distortion: 0.0121 runs per game, against a discrepancy of 2.8 percentage points under investigation. The ceiling fell below the target and the work was stopped. Bounding a mechanism's effect before building it proved the most efficient screening step available.
Fill-conditional price adjustments
Estimated per family, the adjustments reversed sign when pooled, a Simpson's paradox driven by the mix of sides quoted, and the pooled interval spanned 21 percentage points. The conclusion was not that the effect was unproven but that it was not identifiable at the available sample size.
Reduction of the minimum edge threshold
A two-arm walk-forward test returned point estimates favoring the reduction. It was rejected: the fill simulator is known to flatter marginal orders, and the result fell on the side that bias would produce. The interpretation agreed before the test was that a result in the direction of a known bias is not evidence. Fixing the reading in advance, including the conditions under which a favorable result will be rejected, is the only reliable protection against selecting the interpretation after the fact.

6.Measurement basis

The most frequent error encountered in this work was not in any model but in the choice of sample used to evaluate one. Conditioning an evaluation on an event correlated with the outcome produces a biased estimate that additional data does not correct.

An instance from this project: calibration measured only on contracts where a resting order filled. An order fills when a counterparty takes the opposing side, which occurs preferentially when the quoted price is wrong, so conditioning on fills selects for the model's errors and reports worse calibration than the model exhibits. An internal write-up had already published a self-critical finding drawn from that sample. Recomputed on the full quoted tape, expected calibration error is 0.007 against the market's 0.007 on game markets and 0.006 against 0.014 on props, comparable or better; the model's deficiency lies in discrimination, not calibration.

Unfavorable results warrant the same scrutiny as favorable ones. A finding that flatters the analyst is checked as a matter of course; one that does not is often accepted without the same examination, and can be equally wrong.

7.Scope and limitations

The evaluation window covers one partial season, roughly two months of operation and 786 games, and establishes nothing about stability across seasons, rule changes, or roster turnover. The benchmark incorporates information the model does not receive, including late lineup and weather news, so an unknown portion of the measured shortfall reflects an information gap rather than a modeling deficiency. All modeling and interpretive decisions were made by one person; the pre-commitment and placebo procedures described above are a partial substitute for independent review. The simulation engine itself is implemented twice, once as a compiled kernel and once independently from the same specification, and the two are required to agree on the simulated distribution to within Monte Carlo error — which checks the code, though not the judgment applied to it.

Realized trading results are excluded. The accounting routine that produced the figures last computed contained a settlement defect, since corrected, and the sample is in any case far too short to separate return from noise.

The exchange is not identified and no market data is redistributed. All simulation results above are reproducible from the published repository; the benchmark comparison is computed from a private record of quoted prices and is identified as such wherever it appears. The system described is not in operation.
