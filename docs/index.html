A base-out simulation model for baseball outcomes, and its out-of-sample validation

Eli Witz May–August 2026 Source code and full write-up eawitz@ucdavis.edu

Summary

This memo describes a Monte Carlo model that estimates the joint distribution of runs scored in a baseball game from individual player rates, and reports the out-of-sample testing used to assess it. It quoted seven families of game-level contracts continuously on a regulated binary options exchange for two months.

Two questions organize the evaluation. First, does its forecast distribution match what actually happened on the field? Broadly yes: forecast probabilities line up with observed frequencies, and the probability integral transform is close to uniform in every family. Second, is it better than the price it was quoting against? No. Across 35,159 scored contracts covering 764 games, the model's Brier score was 0.1905 against the market's 0.1896, where lower is better. The gap is small enough that the uncertainty around it spans zero. One of the seven families is significantly worse than the market; the other six are indistinguishable from it.

1.Model structure

Why a simulation

Every contract the model priced asks about a tail, not an average. Whether two teams combine for more than 8.5 runs is a question about crossing a threshold. A margin contract requires the distribution of the difference between two correlated quantities. A regression predicting expected runs supplies neither, because getting from an average to the probability of exceeding a threshold requires the shape of the distribution around that average, and a mean regression does not estimate it.

So the model simulates games instead. It plays a half-inning one plate appearance at a time, tracking two things only: which bases are occupied and how many are out. Eight arrangements of runners and three out counts give 24 states[1,2], and the state is Markov: what happens next depends on the current state and the batter, not on how the inning got there. That is what makes the approach practical, because the consequence of every outcome in every state can be worked out once and stored in a table, and a game then runs to completion in microseconds.

The matchup

Each plate appearance draws one of nine outcomes — strikeout, walk, hit by pitch, ground out, fly out, single, double, triple, home run — from probabilities specific to that batter facing that pitcher. Combining a batter's rate and a pitcher's rate into a matchup rate is the log5 problem[5], and the model uses the multinomial generalization of it[6,7,8] rather than the classical binary form, since nine outcomes have to stay coherent with one another. The outcome moves the runners and may score runs. Running a matchup 50,000 times gives the joint distribution of home and away runs, and every price is read off that single object. Deriving that distribution algebraically is the alternative, and it is not available here: plate appearances are neither independent nor identically distributed, since the base-out state carries from one to the next and the batter changes every time.

When the starter comes out, and who replaces him

Two layers on top of this core matter enough to describe. The first is when the starting pitcher is removed, which drives a large share of scoring, because whoever replaces him is usually fresher. The model estimates, at each plate appearance, the probability the starter is pulled before the next one, from pitch count, runs allowed, the score margin and the base-out state. It uses a gradient-boosted tree model[18] rather than a regression because the relationship is neither smooth nor additive, and the same pitch count means something different at a one-run margin than at a six-run one.

The second is which reliever comes in, a choice from a menu whose contents change every night, since the available bullpen depends on who pitched yesterday. A conditional logit[17] handles that, modeling the choice from the characteristics of the options themselves — role, handedness, recent workload, the inning, the score — so it still works when tomorrow's list is different. Drawing relievers at random from a pool would put the wrong arm into the highest-leverage innings and understate how much the best relievers suppress scoring.

Called strikes

This component follows a published model closely rather than adapting one. Whether a taken pitch is called a strike depends on four people at once: the catcher receiving it, the umpire behind him, the pitcher's command of the edge, and the batter's own effective zone. Those contributions have to be separated or each one absorbs the others, and a pitcher throws to the same one or two catchers all season, so his measured skill silently includes their framing. The model estimates the four as crossed effects on a called-strike probability surface, following Deshpande and Wyner's hierarchical Bayesian treatment of pitch framing[11] and the called-strikes-above-average work at Baseball Prospectus[12]. Their result that the umpire effect is the larger one, with the widest zone worth roughly twice the best framer, carries over here, and it is why the umpire is applied at simulation time rather than folded into any pitcher's rate.

Batted balls, and why home runs are modeled differently

One more piece works differently from everything above, in that it acts on the inputs to the simulation rather than inside it. Most of the model runs on rates: a batter has some probability of a home run, estimated from how often he has hit one. That works when the outcome is common and breaks down when it is rare. A batter's doubles rate needs something like 1,600 plate appearances to settle down, and nobody gets that in a season, so the estimate is credibility-weighted nearly all the way to the league average and the model stops telling batters apart.

The way out is to model the ball instead of the rate. Exit velocity and launch angle settle down within 40 to 80 batted balls, and between them they very nearly determine what happens.

So each batter's own distribution of batted balls is integrated through that surface, and through each park's version of it. That last step is what turns a park factor into something batter-specific: the same fly ball clears the fence in one park and is caught in another, and which batters it helps depends on where and how hard they hit the ball. The layer is centered to zero across batters within each park, so it adds only that interaction and leaves the park's average home run rate alone.

The same construction was extended to singles, doubles and triples, and the reason was measurable. Comparing the spread of the model's per-batter estimates against the spread of what actually happened gives a ratio of 0.55 for singles, 0.30 for doubles and 0.12 for triples: on the rare outcomes the model had almost stopped distinguishing between batters. The same ratio for home runs was 0.82, and home runs were the outcome that already had the batted-ball layer underneath.

The rest

Those three in-simulation components are part of eleven modulating layers in all. The remaining eight are times through the order, park factors, platoon splits, the running game, and others.

2.Parameter estimation

The model needs nine outcome probabilities for every batter against every pitcher. The obvious way to get them is to count what each player has actually done, and it does not work. A hitter with 150 plate appearances has a strikeout rate with a standard error of about 3.5 percentage points, larger than most of the differences the model is trying to detect. Taken at face value, those rates would have the simulation reproducing a great deal of noise.

Instead, each player's rate is pulled toward what a model of his observable characteristics predicts for a player like him, by an amount that depends on how much is actually known about him[13]. The estimate is a weighted average of the two:

θ^i = Zi⁢yi + (1−Zi) ⁢mi , Zi= ni ni+k , k= σwithin2 σbetween2

yi is the player's own rate, mi what the covariate model predicts for him, ni his playing time, and k the ratio of within-player noise to between-player spread.

This is the Bühlmann credibility estimator[14], with Z the credibility factor and k the ratio of expected process variance to variance of hypothetical means. When the noise is large relative to the real spread, everyone gets pulled harder: a hitter with a handful of games moves most of the way to the prediction, and one with a full season barely moves at all.

The one departure from the textbook form is that both variance components are estimated alongside the rates rather than fixed in advance, so how much to shrink is part of the answer instead of an assumption. The covariates the prior mean is built from are themselves measured with error, which matters because a slope fitted on a reliable version of a covariate will over-weight a noisy one[15]. Fitting everything at once means sampling from a distribution over many correlated parameters. That is done with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo[16], which uses the gradient of the log posterior to decide where to step next and so keeps exploring efficiently in high dimensions where simpler samplers get stuck.

The simulation can then run on the average of those parameter estimates or on draws from their full distribution. Drawing from the posterior costs run time, and it is worth paying, because the model's output is a non-linear function of the parameters. By Jensen's inequality, for a tail probability g and parameters θ,

E⁢[g⁡(θ)] ≠ g⁡(E⁢[θ])

so the tail probability computed at the average parameter value is not the average tail probability across parameter values. Treating them as equal makes the forecasts look more certain than they are.

3.Calibration against outcomes

Before comparing the model against anything else, the question is whether its probabilities mean what they say. A forecast of 30% should be right about 30% of the time. Figure 6 groups every game-level forecast by the probability it assigned and plots the frequency with which those events actually occurred.

A useless forecaster can pass that test. Quoting the base rate on every game produces perfectly calibrated probabilities that say nothing about any particular game. The sharper question is whether the whole predicted distribution has the right shape, and the test for that is the probability integral transform[22].

The idea is straightforward. For each game, the model's predicted distribution over the outcome, whether total runs, a team's runs or the margin, is evaluated at what actually happened, giving the fraction of that distribution lying below the realized value. If the model is right, that fraction is equally likely to fall anywhere between 0 and 1, so collecting it across hundreds of games should produce a flat histogram. A distribution that is too narrow pushes too many outcomes into the extreme tails and the histogram develops a U shape. One that is too wide bulges in the middle.

All seven come out close to flat, which indicates that the model's uncertainty is about the right width rather than merely centered in the right place. The largest departures are in game totals and the first-five markets, and both are modest.

4.Comparison against the market

Forecasts are scored with the Brier score[20]: the squared difference between the probability quoted and the realized outcome, averaged over every contract, so lower is better.

BS= 1N ∑ i=1 N (pi−yi) 2

pi is the quoted probability, yi is 1 if the event happened and 0 otherwise.

It is strictly proper, meaning a forecaster gets its best expected score only by reporting what it genuinely believes. That matters when comparing two forecasters, because an improper measure such as accuracy or a hit rate can be improved by misreporting.

A forecaster that ignores the specific game and always quotes the base rate scores 0.2490. The model at 0.1905 and the market at 0.1896 are both far better than that, so both carry real information. The difference between the two is 0.0010 in the market's favor, and the game-clustered 95% interval around it runs from −0.0027 to +0.0007. It contains zero, so the two are not distinguishable on this sample. Figure 8 breaks it down by family.

A Brier score splits into three parts: how miscalibrated the forecasts are, how well they discriminate between events that happen and events that do not, and the irreducible uncertainty of the events themselves. Doing that split by binning is unstable, since the answer moves with the choice of bins. Doing it by isotonic regression is not[25]. Applied to all seven families:

modelmarketdifference [95% CI]
miscalibration0.220.24−0.01 [−0.16, +0.16]
discrimination59.0459.91−0.87 [−2.51, +0.76]
uncertainty248.98248.98—

All figures ×10⁻³. Brier score = miscalibration − discrimination + uncertainty, so better discrimination lowers the score.

That settles which of the two questions the model loses on. The calibration difference is a hundredth of a point with a third of a point of interval around it, so the two are calibrated equally well, and that is a tight statement rather than a noisy one. Both are well calibrated in absolute terms: miscalibration costs the model 0.22 against an irreducible 249, under a tenth of a percent of its score. Nearly the whole 0.95 gap is discrimination instead, though the interval on it contains zero, so the direction is clear and the size is not established.

One family, game totals, is significantly worse on the Brier score; the other six are indistinguishable from the market. The weakness in totals concentrates at high lines rather than spreading evenly, which locates it as a specific modeling defect rather than as evidence that the family is efficiently priced. It is also the family whose probability integral transform in Figure 7 departs furthest from flat.

A more direct test is what happens when the two disagree. On the 1,692 contracts where the model and the market took opposite sides, the model's side won 48.8% of the time, which is a coin flip. Its stated confidence is honest and its distribution matches reality, but it cannot separate the games the market has mispriced from the ones it has not. That second ability is called discrimination[21], and it is the one the model is missing.

5.Rejected model changes

A list of changes that worked says little, because it was selected on having worked. Each of the following was built into the simulation, measured against a criterion fixed before the test ran, and then abandoned.

Correcting for compression in the strikeout estimates
Pitchers' estimated strikeout rates appeared to be shrunk too far toward the league average, so a multiplier was fitted to push them back out and was shipped. A direct on/off comparison then showed it made the probabilistic forecasts worse at every level, starter through game, while improving the average predicted count, which is the signature of an over-correction. The reason was that the compression being corrected did not exist. How much shrinkage the estimates appear to have depends entirely on how it is measured: logits of noisy single-game rates give a slope of 1.21, per-game rates give 0.89, and proper aggregation by pitcher gives 1.04, which is calibrated. The multiplier was turned off and the whole line of work stopped.
Per-batter platoon splits inside the model fit
Every batter has some individual tendency to hit left-handed and right-handed pitching differently, and that was moved from a post-hoc adjustment into the model's own likelihood. Graded over 531 walk-forward games it came out at parity or slightly worse, with the correlation to realized starter strikeouts falling from 0.496 to 0.489. The per-player signal is real and was measured correctly; once properly shrunk it is simply too small to matter across a whole game.
Weighting recent performance more heavily for pitchers who have changed
A gate that up-weighted recent starts for pitchers whose velocity or swing-and-miss rate had moved lost to a fixed middling weight, in every third of every measure of change, including pitchers whose stuff had demonstrably shifted. Leaning on recent data over-weights noise even when the change is real. The premise held up, though — recent change does predict forward error — but it belongs in the model of what a pitcher's stuff implies about his strikeout rate, not in how his history is weighted.
Position players pitching
Position players occasionally pitch in lopsided games, and the model treated them as ordinary relievers. Before building the correction, the largest effect it could possibly have was computed as the share of plate appearances affected multiplied by the biggest plausible rate distortion: 0.0121 runs per game, against a discrepancy of 2.8 percentage points under investigation. The ceiling was below the target, making the mechanism immaterial by construction, so the work stopped there. Bounding a mechanism's effect before building it was the cheapest screening step available and the one that saved the most time.

The first is the one worth dwelling on: a correction for a defect that turned out to be an artifact of how the defect had been measured. That is the subject of the next section.

6.Measurement basis

The most frequent error in this project was never in a model. It was in the choice of sample used to judge one. Evaluating a model only on cases selected by something correlated with the answer produces a result that is biased rather than merely noisy, and collecting more data does not correct it.

An instance from this project: calibration measured only on contracts where a resting order filled. An order fills when a counterparty takes the opposing side, which happens preferentially when the quoted price is wrong. Fills are adversely selected, so conditioning on them selects for the model's own errors and reports worse calibration than the model exhibits. An earlier write-up of this work had already reported a self-critical finding drawn from that sample. Recomputed on the full quoted tape, the two forecasters are calibrated equally well, which is the result reported in section 4. What the model lacks is discrimination, and the evidence for that is the head-to-head record rather than anything visible in Figure 6.

Unfavorable results deserve the same scrutiny as favorable ones. A finding that flatters the analyst is checked as a matter of course; one that does not is often accepted without the same examination, and can be equally wrong.

7.Scope and limitations

The evaluation window covers one partial season, roughly two months of operation and 764 games, and establishes nothing about stability across seasons, rule changes, or roster turnover. The benchmark incorporates information the model does not receive, including late lineup and weather news, so an unknown portion of the measured shortfall reflects an information gap rather than a modeling deficiency. All modeling and interpretive decisions were made by one person; fixing each test's criterion before running it, as in section 5, is a partial substitute for independent review.
