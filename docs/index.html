Technical memo

A base-out simulation model for baseball outcomes, and its out-of-sample validation

Eli Witz May–August 2026 Source code and full write-up

Summary

This memo describes a Monte Carlo model that estimates the joint distribution of runs scored in a baseball game from individual player rates, and reports the out-of-sample testing used to assess it. The model quoted six families of game-level contracts on a regulated binary options exchange, continuously, for two months.

It gets asked two questions. First, does its forecast distribution match what actually happened on the field? Broadly yes: calibration is close through the middle of the range and the simulated distribution of run scoring tracks the realized one across the whole ladder of totals. Second, is it better than the price it was quoting against? No. Across 24,659 scored contracts covering 760 games it scored 0.1885 on the Brier score, a measure of forecast error where lower is better, against the market's 0.1875. The gap is small enough that the uncertainty around it spans zero. One of the six families is significantly worse than the market; the other five are indistinguishable from it.

1.Model structure

Every contract the model priced asks about a tail, not an average. Whether two teams combine for more than 8.5 runs is a question about the probability of crossing a threshold; a margin contract asks for the distribution of the difference between two correlated quantities. A regression predicting expected runs answers neither, because getting from an average to the probability of exceeding a threshold requires the shape of the distribution around that average, and a regression never estimates it.

So the model simulates games instead. It plays a half-inning one plate appearance at a time, tracking two things only: which bases are occupied and how many are out. Eight arrangements of runners and three out counts give 24 states, and those 24 describe the situation completely — what happens next depends on the current state and the batter, not on how the inning got there. That is what makes the approach practical. The consequences of every outcome in every state can be worked out once and stored in a table, so a game runs to completion in microseconds rather than being re-derived from scratch.

Each plate appearance draws one of nine outcomes — strikeout, walk, hit by pitch, ground out, fly out, single, double, triple, home run — from probabilities specific to that batter facing that pitcher. The outcome moves the runners and may score runs. Running a matchup 50,000 times gives the joint distribution of home and away runs, and every price is read off that one distribution. That is what keeps the prices consistent with each other: the total and the margin cannot end up implying different things about the same game.

The alternative is to derive the distribution algebraically rather than by simulation. That is not available here, because the plate appearances are neither independent nor identical — the base-out state carries from one to the next, and the batter changes every time.

Two components layered on top of that core matter enough to describe. The first is when the starting pitcher is removed, which drives a large share of scoring, because whoever replaces him is usually fresher. The model estimates, at each plate appearance, the probability the starter is pulled before the next one, from pitch count, runs allowed, the score margin and the base-out state. It uses a gradient-boosted tree model rather than a regression because the relationship is neither smooth nor additive: the same pitch count means something different at a one-run margin than at a six-run one, and a method that must be told the functional form in advance would miss that interaction.

The second is which reliever comes in, a choice from a menu whose contents change every night, since the available bullpen depends on who pitched yesterday. A conditional logit handles that. It models the probability of selecting each option from the characteristics of the options themselves — role, handedness, recent workload, the inning, the score — rather than from a fixed list of alternatives, so it still works when the list is different tomorrow. Drawing relievers at random from a pool, the simpler alternative, would put the wrong arm into the highest-leverage innings and understate how much the best relievers suppress scoring.

Nine smaller effects sit above these: times through the order, park and umpire effects, platoon splits, the running game, and others.

2.Parameter estimation

The model needs nine outcome probabilities for every batter against every pitcher. The obvious way to get them is to count what each player has actually done, and it does not work. A hitter with 150 plate appearances has a strikeout rate with a standard error of about 3.5 percentage points, which is larger than most of the differences the model is trying to detect. Take those rates at face value and the simulation will faithfully reproduce a great deal of noise.

Instead, each player's rate is pulled toward what a model of his observable characteristics predicts for a player like him, by an amount that depends on how much is actually known about him. The weight on his own record is his playing time divided by playing time plus a variance ratio. That ratio compares how much players genuinely differ from one another against how noisy each individual estimate is: when the noise is large relative to the real spread, everyone gets pulled harder. In practice a hitter with a handful of games moves most of the way to the prediction, and one with a full season barely moves at all.

Those variance terms are estimated alongside the rates rather than fixed beforehand, so how much to shrink is part of the answer instead of an assumption. Fitting everything at once means sampling from a distribution over many correlated parameters, which is done with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo — a method that uses the slope of the distribution to decide where to step next, and so keeps exploring efficiently in high dimensions where simpler samplers get stuck.

The simulation can then run on the average of those parameter estimates or on draws from their full distribution. Using draws costs time, and it matters: the model's output is a tail probability, and the tail probability computed at the average parameter value is not the same as the average tail probability across parameter values. Treating them as equal makes the forecasts look more certain than they are.

3.Calibration against outcomes

Before comparing the model against anything else, the question is whether its probabilities mean what they say. A forecast of 30% should be right about 30% of the time. 4.Comparison against the market

Forecasts are scored with the Brier score: the squared distance between the probability quoted and what actually happened, averaged over every contract, so lower is better. It has the property of being proper, meaning a forecaster gets its best expected score only by reporting what it genuinely believes. That matters when comparing two forecasters, because an improper measure — accuracy, or a hit rate — can be improved by shading your answers, and would reward the wrong thing.

A forecaster that ignores the specific game and always quotes the base rate scores 0.2489. The model at 0.1885 and the market at 0.1875 are both far better than that, so both carry real information. The difference between the two is 0.0010 in the market's favor, and the game-clustered 95% interval around it runs from −0.0027 to +0.0006 — it contains zero, so the two are not distinguishable on this sample. Figure 3 breaks it down by family.

One family, game totals, is significantly worse; the other five are indistinguishable from the market. The weakness in totals concentrates at high lines rather than spreading evenly, which locates it as a specific modeling defect rather than as evidence that the family is efficiently priced — and it is consistent with the understatement of scoring visible in Figure 2.

A more direct test is what happens when the two disagree. On the 1,229 contracts where the model and the market took opposite sides, the model's side won 49.0% of the time. So its stated confidence is honest and its distribution matches reality, but it cannot separate the games the market has mispriced from the ones it has not. That second ability is called discrimination, and it is the one the model is missing.

5.Sensitivity to the base-running assumption

How base runners advance is the largest single lever on simulated scoring. The published code builds those transition tables three ways and measures each against real league scoring, rather than assuming the simpler version is good enough.

The deterministic treatment understates scoring by 21%, being unable to represent a double play, a sacrifice fly, or a runner scoring from second on a single. Eight sourced constants recover 68% of the shortfall; the residual 6% consists of rare events that no compact parameterization represents.

Most of the recovered difference comes from conditioning these rates on the out count. A runner scores from second on a single 39% of the time with none out and 80% with two out, since with two out he advances on contact. The commonly cited single figure of 63% is an average over out states, and applying it uniformly — as an earlier version of this code did — biases the run distribution in opposite directions depending on the state.

6.Rejected model changes

A list of changes that worked says little, because it was selected on having worked. Each of the following was built into the simulation, measured against a criterion fixed before the test ran, and then abandoned.

Correcting for compression in the strikeout estimates
Pitchers' estimated strikeout rates appeared to be shrunk too far toward the league average, so a multiplier was fitted to push them back out and was shipped. A direct on/off comparison then showed it made the probabilistic forecasts worse at every level — starter, team, batter and game — while improving the average predicted count, which is the signature of an over-correction. The reason was that the compression being corrected did not exist. How much shrinkage the estimates appear to have depends entirely on how you measure it: taking logits of noisy single-game rates gives a slope of 1.21, per-game rates give 0.89, and aggregating properly by pitcher gives 1.04, which is calibrated. The multiplier was turned off and the whole line of work stopped.
Per-batter platoon splits inside the model fit
Every batter has some individual tendency to hit left-handed and right-handed pitching differently. That was moved from a post-hoc adjustment into the model's own likelihood, with a per-player term sampled alongside everything else. Graded over 531 walk-forward games it came out at parity or slightly worse: the correlation with realized starter strikeouts fell from 0.496 to 0.489, and the ability to separate outcomes was lower at every strikeout line — the opposite of what a genuine improvement looks like. The per-player signal is real and was measured correctly; it is simply too small, once properly shrunk, to matter at the level of a whole game.
Weighting recent performance more heavily for pitchers who have changed
The premise was that a pitcher whose velocity or swing-and-miss rate has recently moved should be forecast with more weight on his recent starts. A gate was fitted to do exactly that, and it lost to a fixed middling weight — not only overall but in every third of every measure of change, including for pitchers whose stuff had demonstrably shifted. Leaning on recent data over-weights noise even when the change is real. The premise itself held up, though: recent change does predict forward error. It belongs in the model of what a pitcher's stuff implies about his strikeout rate, not in how his history is weighted, and that is where the work went next.
Position players pitching
Position players occasionally pitch in lopsided games, and the model treated them as ordinary relievers. Before building the correction, the largest effect it could possibly have was computed as the share of plate appearances affected multiplied by the biggest plausible rate distortion: 0.0121 runs per game, against a discrepancy of 2.8 percentage points under investigation. The ceiling was below the target, so the work stopped there. Bounding a mechanism's effect before building it was the cheapest screening step available and the one that saved the most time.

The first of these is the one worth dwelling on. It was not a feature that failed to help; it was a correction for a defect that turned out to be an artifact of how the defect had been measured. That is the subject of the next section.

7.Measurement basis

The most frequent error in this project was never in a model. It was in the choice of sample used to judge one. If you evaluate a model only on cases selected by something that correlates with the answer, the result is biased rather than merely noisy, and collecting more data does not fix it.

An instance from this project: calibration measured only on contracts where a resting order filled. An order fills when a counterparty takes the opposing side, which occurs preferentially when the quoted price is wrong, so conditioning on fills selects for the model's errors and reports worse calibration than the model exhibits. An internal write-up had already published a self-critical finding drawn from that sample. Recomputed on the full quoted tape, expected calibration error is 0.0078 against the market's 0.0071 — close enough that calibration is not where the model loses. What it lacks is discrimination, as Figure 1 and the head-to-head comparison both indicate.

Unfavorable results deserve the same scrutiny as favorable ones. A finding that flatters the analyst is checked as a matter of course; one that does not is often accepted without the same examination, and can be equally wrong.

8.Scope and limitations

The evaluation window covers one partial season, roughly two months of operation and 760 games, and establishes nothing about stability across seasons, rule changes, or roster turnover. The benchmark incorporates information the model does not receive, including late lineup and weather news, so an unknown portion of the measured shortfall reflects an information gap rather than a modeling deficiency. All modeling and interpretive decisions were made by one person; fixing each test's criterion before running it, as in section 6, is a partial substitute for independent review.

Realized trading results are excluded. The accounting routine that produced the figures last computed contained a settlement defect, since corrected, and the sample is in any case far too short to separate return from noise.

The exchange is not identified and no market data is redistributed. All simulation results above are reproducible from the published repository; the benchmark comparison is computed from a private record of quoted prices and is identified as such wherever it appears. The system described is not in operation.
